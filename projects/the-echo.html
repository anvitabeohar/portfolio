The Echo

A psychological thriller in Twine, where your own memories start to lie.

TL;DR · 30-second read

The idea

What if the Mandela Effect were real? A first-person thriller about memory, paranoia and reality manipulation.

What I did

Wrote, designed and built the game solo in Twine, composed the ambient score in Reaper, and drew the line-art illustrations.

Result

A playable branch-and-bottleneck story with two ambiguous endings, published on itch.io.

Premise

Sarah, an introspective sceptic, starts noticing small inconsistencies in her memories. Pulling on that thread uncovers reality-manipulation experiments, and she tells her story to the player as a warning.

Two characters shape her path: Dr. Chen, the woman in a video who first draws her in, and Ethan, a friend who tried to warn her and wasn't believed. Looking back on those ignored warnings gives the story its emotional weight.

Why this story

I've always been interested in how digital media shapes what people believe. Once you engage with a topic, algorithms keep resurfacing it until you're deep in a rabbit hole. Mandela Effect theories did that to me: I clearly remembered details that others remembered differently. If many people share the same "wrong" memory, is it really just misremembering?

That question, fed by online discussions, forums and podcasts, became the foundation of The Echo.

Narrative structure

The story uses a branch-and-bottleneck model. Early choices act like a sorting hat, steering the player towards one of two endings, while key experiences such as "the memory of the lake" are shared by every path.

The story's shape goes from bad to worse. The endings ask whether Sarah is living in a government-controlled simulation, or whether alternate realities are merging and causing the anomalies.

Key decisions

1. Choices live inside the prose

Decision
Links are woven into sentences instead of listed as options underneath, inspired by the interactive story My Father's Long, Long Legs.
Why
Choosing feels like thinking, not like picking from a menu, which keeps the player inside Sarah's head.

2. No prologue

Decision
The game opens mid-story with no introduction.
Why
Players should feel they've stumbled on something they weren't meant to find.

3. No way to win

Decision
Both endings are ambiguous, with no triumphant resolution.
Why
The themes, uncertain memory and malleable truth, don't allow a clean victory. Ambiguity lets the unease linger after the game ends.

Look and sound

  • Minimal interface: white text on a dark background keeps attention on atmosphere and story.
  • Clear choices: links are larger than body text and glow blue on hover, so they're easy to spot.
  • Typography as tone: Roboto for the narrative; Orbitron in key passages for a robotic, disconnected feel.
  • Digital distortion: glow and RGB-split shadows on selected sentences reflect the story's digital nature.
  • Sound: an ambient synth hum I made in Reaper grows more intense as the story progresses.
  • Illustration: handmade minimalist line art appears like faint impressions in a dream.

Why it matters for UX

  • Signifiers. Size and hover colour made choices obvious without breaking immersion, the same balance any interface needs between clarity and flow.
  • Flow mapping. Branch-and-bottleneck is a user-flow problem: many paths, a few shared moments, and states that must stay consistent.
  • Feedback over time. The rising hum tells players tension is building without a single word, like progressive feedback in a product.
