Context

The International Olympic Committee launched the Olympic Virtual Series in 2021, and the first Olympic Esports Games are now planned for 2027 in Riyadh. But almost every VR event on the programme is a digital copy of an existing sport. VR Taekwondo works, yet it treats VR as a simulator rather than a medium with its own physical, cognitive and spatial possibilities.

The question: can a game designed for VR meet the bar the Olympics set for athleticism, strategic depth and safety? I used Beat Saber in 360° mode as a test case: it combines rhythm, full-body movement and spatial awareness, is culturally neutral, and is the best-selling VR exergame.

My role

  • Study design: research questions, a power analysis to set the sample size, and a four-session protocol.
  • Ethics and recruitment: approval from Trinity's Human Research Ethics Committee, and recruitment through mailing lists, social media and campus flyers.
  • Running the study: briefing, headset fitting and biometric tracking for all 29 participants, with no drop-outs.
  • Analysis and write-up: t-tests, correlations and regressions, then turning results into design principles.

Method

A power analysis showed at least 27 participants were needed to detect a medium effect. I recruited 29 adults aged 20–30 (17 women, 12 men); 48% had substantial VR experience. Each person wore a Meta Quest 3 and a Samsung Galaxy Watch 4, and rated their effort on the Borg scale after every song.

Surveys before and after covered prior VR and fitness habits, comfort, immersion, perceived improvement, and whether participants saw the game as a legitimate sport. Each session took 40–45 minutes and all data was anonymised at collection.

Key findings

1. It's real exercise

Finding
Average heart rate climbed from 114 to 122 to 124 bpm across the three measured sessions, each far above a 70 bpm resting baseline (p < .001, Cohen's d 2.55–3.04).
So what
That's the range of table tennis and above precision Olympic sports like archery and shooting, so the game clears the bar for "physical prowess."

2. Players stop noticing the effort

Finding
Heart rate and perceived effort were moderately linked in the first measured session (ρ = .44, p = .017), but the link disappeared by the hard session (ρ = .10).
So what
As players settle into rhythm and flow, self-reports stop tracking what their bodies are doing. Competitive VR needs biometric monitoring, not just questionnaires.

3. Effort and thinking trade off

Finding
Higher physical effort early on went with lower mental engagement (ρ = −.37, p = .05).
So what
Mechanics have to balance strategic depth with physical load, or one crowds out the other.

4. Safe for most first-timers

Finding
65.5% reported no adverse effects; the rest had mild dizziness or nausea that passed without help. Fatigue didn't differ between novices and experienced players (13.3% vs 7.1%, p = 1.0).
So what
VR sickness is manageable, but Olympic formats would still need session limits and ergonomic rules.

Design principles

I turned the findings and the literature into six principles for building VR-native sports that could meet Olympic criteria for legitimacy, fairness and inclusion.

01

Move beyond physical limits

Use VR's 3D spatial freedom for movement the real world can't offer, like 360° play spaces and multidimensional navigation.

02

Match Olympic physical benchmarks

Reward motor skill, precision and stamina, not button-pressing, so the sport is genuinely athletic.

03

Fit diverse bodies, keep it fair

Scale motion and difficulty for different physiologies without breaking the universality of competition.

04

Govern transparently

Independent oversight for anti-cheat, hardware certification and athlete welfare, including session limits for VR sickness.

05

No pay-to-win

Standardise hardware thresholds, such as headset latency of 20 ms or less and refresh rates of 90 Hz or more.

06

Design for spectators

Dynamic cameras, heart-rate overlays and clear narratives so viewers can follow what players feel.

Why it matters for UX

  • Measure behaviour, not just opinions. Participants' effort ratings drifted away from their heart rates. In product work, I pair what people say with what they actually do.
  • Cognitive load is a budget. Physical effort crowded out mental engagement. Dense enterprise workflows have the same trade-off between task load and thinking.
  • Design for first-timers without boring experts. The familiarisation round let novices learn before anything was measured, much like good onboarding.

Reflection

Limitations. One session per person, healthy young adults rather than elite athletes, and a single headset. The results show potential, not proof of Olympic readiness.

Next steps

  • Follow top-ranked players over months to measure long-term skill growth.
  • Test performance parity across headsets, including latency and tracking differences.
  • Measure VR-specific fatigue, such as cognitive load and hand-eye coordination over long sessions.
